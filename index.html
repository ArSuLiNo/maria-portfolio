Scimatch

Making a science-first dating app legible to people who don't read science.

Situation

Scimatch matched people on facial physiognomy rather than swipes and bios. Genuinely differentiated — and genuinely hard to explain. Against Tinder and Bumble, "we read your face" reads as either a gimmick or a privacy problem unless the reasoning is made visible.

What I owned

Positioning and the content engine: how the mechanic gets explained, in what order, and through which formats — plus influencer distribution.

What I did

  • Reframed the pitch from feature ("face recognition") to promise ("the face never lies") so the first line sells the outcome, not the tech.
  • Built a post architecture that teaches the mechanic in three beats: why not bios → what a face reveals → how the algorithm reads it.
  • Used the product's own visual language — landmark overlays on portraits — so every asset demonstrates the mechanic instead of describing it.
  • Ran influencer outreach across a 300+ contact pipeline to seed the explanation with people users already trust.

Outcome

Posts carrying the mechanic outperformed generic lifestyle content — the explainer format reached 1,710 likes against a ~930 baseline. The three-beat structure became the default template for the channel.

VERGE

10,000 followers in six weeks, with no paid budget.

Situation

An independent clothing brand with a product people liked and an audience that didn't know it existed. No ad spend, no agency, a six-week window before the drop.

What I owned

The whole growth loop: what we made, when it went out, what we measured, and what we killed.

What I did

  • Treated the feed as a testing surface — small batches of formats shipped against a fixed cadence instead of one big campaign bet.
  • Read engagement data weekly and reallocated production time toward the formats that were compounding, not the ones we liked most.
  • Produced the photography in-house, which cut the cost of an experiment to near zero and made fast iteration affordable.
  • Worked directly with brand leadership on positioning and audience targeting so growth and identity moved together.

Outcome

+10,000 organic followers in six weeks. More usefully: a repeatable format library and a cadence the brand could keep running without me.

Three landing pages, three markets

Same job to be done, three completely different audiences.

Situation

A coffee shop in Dubai, a yoga studio in Bali and an events club in Moscow. All small teams, all needing the same thing: a page that turns interest into a booking, a visit or a membership.

What I owned

End to end — scoping, structure, copy, visual direction and build. Shipped live, not mocked up.

What I did

  • Started each build from the decision the visitor is trying to make, then cut every section that didn't serve it.
  • Wrote structure before visuals — for MAYAK, membership proof (300 members) sits directly above the join action, because the objection is social, not informational.
  • Matched interface tone to context: Surf runs loud and yellow for walk-in appetite; Bali Bliss runs quiet and typographic for a considered purchase.
  • Designed mobile-first, since all three audiences arrive from a phone via social.

Outcome

Three products live and independently maintainable by their owners. Concept to launch measured in days, not sprints.

Three publications, hard deadlines, no authority over anyone.

Situation

STRIKE, Off the Cuff and The BU Buzz ship on fixed publication dates. Every issue means concept, talent, styling, shoot, edit and delivery — run by people who don't report to you.

What I owned

Production end to end: brief, schedule, run-of-show, final asset selection and handoff.

What I did

Outcome

20+ published placements across three publications, every issue delivered on date. The transferable part isn't the photography — it's shipping on a fixed date with a team you influence rather than manage.

Approach

How I actually work

Not a framework I read about. This is the loop underneath every case above.

  1. 01

    Find the real constraint

    Most briefs describe a symptom. Scimatch didn't have a content problem, it had a legibility problem. Naming the actual constraint changes what you build.

  2. 02

    Decide before you produce

    Structure, cadence and success measure get agreed while they're still cheap to change. Production is the most expensive place to have an opinion.

  3. 03

    Ship small, ship often

    Small batches against a fixed cadence beat one large bet. It generates evidence weekly instead of a verdict quarterly.

  4. 04

    Cut what isn't compounding

    Read the data, kill the formats that flatline, move the time into what's working. The hard part is killing the thing you're proud of.
